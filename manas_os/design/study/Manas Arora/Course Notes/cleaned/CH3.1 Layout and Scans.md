## Weekly Frame

### 10-Week Simple Moving Average

This line is the 10-week simple moving average line. There is another line right at the bottom on volume — this is the 10-week average line on volume. When the volume is above this line, it means the volume is above the average of the last 10 weeks. When the volume is below this line, it means the volume is way below the average of the last 10 weeks.

### Three Key Numbers

I have three values displayed here in colour.

**Green number — % up from 52-week low:** The green one says 57.69 — that is how much the stock is up from its 52-week low. I don't have to do this calculation every time; I can simply look at this number. Why do I have this number here? Because logically, if the stock is up 57%, that means the stock has a lot of buying power. And because I'm looking for stocks in strong momentum, that's your first sign.

Similarly, this one is 46–47% from its 52-week low. This one is also 43% up. This is 55. When you have a lot of options — say 20 names in your watchlist and you're confused how to eliminate the bad ones — this is one objective way to delete names that don't have strong momentum.

**Second number — % from 52-week high:** This number is how much the stock is away from its 52-week high. If I calculate from here, it is exactly 13.72 — and this is what this number says. Again, it saves me from calculating every time, and I can just look at this number and make a better watchlist.

**Yellow number — distance between price and 10-week MA:** The third number in yellow is the distance between the price and the 10-week moving average. In this name, right now the distance between the current price and the 10-week moving average is 34%. Now, why this matters: if you see this is a stock which has been running up for so many days, if the distance between the price and this line is way too much, the upside is limited in my view. Then we do not take that stock, no matter how tempting the setup looks.

You will ask: how much is that number? What is a good number? Every stock has its own nature. Large names with large market cap don't really move that fast. If you just study the history — you can see that this stock moves really fast. At this point in the past, it went up 48.62%; the stock reversed. It did not really progress for the next 5 weeks. Now the stock is up 34%. So this is the nature of the stock.

I'll show you a big name: this is a very large name with a very high market cap. You can see that at the sharpest rally, the distance was 17%, and then the stock reversed. Because it's a large name, it does not fly that fast — so the distance between the price and the line never goes to 40–50%. Whereas in a smaller company — right now in a very strong market — if you see, this is 29% already, and in the same phase the same stock was 48% away from the averages. In short, smaller stocks fly faster and their distance between the line and current price is usually in the upper 30s and 40s; the bigger ones don't really go that much.

How do I really use this number? If the distance between the price and the current line is historically on a very high side, I will not consider that stock. I ideally want the distance to be on the lower side — like 8%, 9%, 10% is acceptable; 12% is acceptable. But if it was already 25%, 30%, 40%, I'm not touching that stock for sure. I would wait for the line to catch up, the distance to come down, and then I'll consider it again. Just this information can save me from taking trades in good stocks at the wrong time.

## Daily Frame

On the daily frame I have three lines:
- **Blue:** 10-day simple moving average
- **Second:** 20-day simple moving average
- **Green:** 50-day simple moving average

Now, 90% of the time I don't have the 10 and 50 turned on. I'm simply working with the 20-day MA to spot my setups. I'll explain in the coming chapters exactly when I really use the 10 and 50.

Again on the daily I also have this moving average on volume — a 50-day moving average on volume. Why 50? Because on the weekly we had 10 weeks, and every week has five trading days. 10 × 5 = 50 days. When a bar is above this line, it means the volume is way above average.

### Purple Dot Tool

The purple dot is my main — the most important — tool on the daily frame. Every stock has a different market cap: large caps, mid caps, and small caps.

Smaller cap stocks have very low free float. Free float is the number of shares in circulation in the market. If you have less supply and there is good demand, the price will obviously explode faster. In larger names, because the number of shares in the market is too many, it requires a huge demand for the price to really explode. That is why we don't really see big names exploding.

Because the name of the course is "Fast and Fortunate: How to Grow Your Small Account Fast," you obviously have to be invested in stocks that really move fast. If you invest in big, familiar names, they will not really move your account fast.

How do you find out which stocks really move fast and which ones are slow? You don't have to check the market cap every time before you take a trade. You can simply plot this tool, which creates a purple dot when the stock moves more than 5% in either direction on more than half a million (5 lakh) volume. If Elecon (Corrected, raw: "Electra") goes up more than 5% — or goes down 5% — on more than 5 lakh shares, there will be a purple dot there.

If you notice Elecon has gone up 7% on almost 10 million volume on a day — which is why we have this dot here. If you see the past when we started to have so many purple dots, that's a fact: Elecon moves really fast and it brings in a lot of volume.

If I show you a large name — how many dots do you see? Not even one. Not even one, which means Reliance does not really move 5% in one day. If you stay invested in Reliance, you're not going to grow your account fast. This is why these dots are really important. When I'm making my watchlist, if I come across a great setup but it does not have any dot, then I will skip it because I don't want to be in a slow-moving stock.

## Relative Strength Layout (Two-Chart Layout)

There is one more layout. If you go here and select two charts, it opens two stocks side by side. On top you have the index — the mid-cap / small-cap index. The idea of stacking two different charts on top of each other is to compare. I'm trying to find relative strength.

Relative strength means: how is your stock doing in comparison to the index? Is it doing any better? Is it doing worse? Or is it just moving with the market? If it is doing better, that's called relative strength. That's how I measure it.

This tool is more helpful in bad markets to find the future leaders. If you notice, we were in a really bad trend for almost the entire last year — mainly from December to March 2023 we were in a really big downtrend. In the same period, especially in the last phase from Feb 23 to March 23, RVNL (Corrected, raw: "VNL") was in an uptrend. Clearly the opposite picture: you have a bad market where markets are making new lows, and here you have a stock making new highs. That's how I measure relative strength. I put these stocks in my focus list and keep tracking them.

When the tide turns and the market also turns, I know that these stocks will really fly and do better than the market. You can see the stock just exploded. By 5th May, the stock was already up 100%. The stock had doubled in just no time.

This layout is very helpful in bad markets. Relative strength is your best friend when markets are really bad. You can really spot future leaders by just simply comparing — no numbers, no RS rating, no "85 and above" or "90 and above." As long as you can see good behavior — the stock is in an uptrend when the markets are down — that's your future leader.

Again, we will learn in detail how to spot RS in the coming chapters. For now this is the layout I have: the index here and then I compare my stocks with the index to find future leaders.
