# CH 2.3: Market Cycles and Stage Analysis

The next important information that you need to really remember is market cycles and how a stock can go through these four stages. This information can really help you in identifying where we stand in the trend: whether we are really early in the trend, the trend is really topping out, or it's too late already and the upside is limited.

## The Four Stages — Example 1

This is a weekly chart of a very popular stock [⚠ unverified ticker — context says "a very popular stock in London from 2018"; ticker not stated in transcript] from 2018. If you see this, it was absolutely in no trend. If I draw a box here, it was sideways and the moving averages were also not really trending.

All of a sudden — this period is 7 years. This looks like a very small box but this is 7 years — something happened. All of a sudden the stock started trending and the stock just went up from 150 to 5,000 in only — this is Jan 17 and this is August 18 — a period of only 15–16 months. The stock was up some 30 times. It was dead for 7 years and then it went up 30 times in only 15–16 months.

After that it went sideways for a while. Then price collapsed: it broke both moving averages and had a sharp fall — almost from 5,000 back to almost where it started, around 400. This is a complete cycle. This is what I'm talking about: Stage 1, Stage 2, Stage 3, and Stage 4.

- **Stage 1:** No trend for many months or even many years — the basing period.
- **Stage 2:** The price crosses both averages. The shorter moving average is above the longer one, the price is trending up, and you see an explosive rise — the stock usually goes 2–3×, 10–20×, or even 30× as in this case.
- **Stage 3:** The price has already gone up a lot and is no longer progressing much. It becomes really choppy. The largest rise was from Jan 17 to Jan 18; after that from Jan 18 to November 18 the price did not really progress much. It's not really the best time to buy.
- **Stage 4:** The price collapses — down almost 80–90%.

## The Four Stages — Example 2

I can give you one more example. This is another weekly chart. Stage 1 is where the stock had no trend and was just forming a base. The prices were going above and below the averages, and then the range breakout happened.

Stage 2: price started going up rapidly. The averages are trending up and the prices are advancing really fast. The stock goes from 42 to 700 in only two years. In only two years the price went up from 40 to 700.

After that, the price started to top out. Finally the price kept going below the averages convincingly, and then Stage 4 set in — the price collapsed back to where it started, almost 80–90%. Again a complete cycle: base (Stage 1), advance (Stage 2), top-out formation (Stage 3), rapid decline (Stage 4).

## We Only Care About Stage 2

We do not have to identify Stage 1, Stage 3, or Stage 4. We are just concerned with Stage 2, because that is where you make money the fastest. You can see the stock went up from 50 to 700. This is exactly where you can grow your account really fast.

For example, right now let's say this is today. We are definitely not in Stage 2 because the averages are not in a clear trend and the price is sometimes going up, sometimes going down. Suddenly for 4–5 weeks the price went up like this — now I am convinced, I can say for sure that we are in Stage 2 because the price has broken out and the trend averages are trending up. That's all you want to know.

Our job is to find Stage 2 stocks, because that's where the biggest money is. You can make really big money in a very short amount of time if you buy young-Stage-2 stocks and stay with them.

## Base Count — How Young Is the Trend?

The next thing that you need to really keep in mind is what happens in Stage 2. This is the chart of JSL and this is 2016. If you see, the price was really not going anywhere in this space — that's your Stage 1. Once it started going higher convincingly, we are in the uptrend. The stock went up almost from 18 to 106, and this is August 16 to November 17 — about one year — almost six or seven times.

Pay attention to what happened in Stage 2, because that's all we're concerned about. That's all I want you to learn and understand.

This is the rally — this is the Stage 2 start. Let's count the resting periods, because that is what I want you to pay attention to.

This is your first move that started here, and then this is your **first resting period** — that's your first base. After that the stock went up again and formed the base again — that's your **second base**. Then the stock spent some more time for many weeks doing nothing — that's your **third base**. Then it takes off and forms a fourth base, but it never breaks out of this base and just collapses. That's your Stage 4.

What I'm trying to show you here is that in Stage 2, whenever there is a rally — if you can count — in some rallies this counting is very easy and it's very obvious. You can just simply count and see where we are: first, second, third. After the fourth base, in fact the fourth base breakout never happened. This is the third and the stock just collapsed after this. There is a false breakout — it broke out this week but never really moved higher, just collapsed.

## How Base Count Helps

How is this information helpful? If you can count, and if the base count is really obvious, and the stock is already three bases old — the upside after that is very limited, and a lot of false breakouts are possible.

If I'm here at this point and this is today, I know that this rally is already three bases old. And I know that from here, if I'm playing the stock, I will not play aggressively. I'll play small, or I will not play at all. That's my trading approach.

This is why the base count is really important. You can find out how early you are in the trend. If you're here — only two bases old — that's just the start of the move. If the stock has legs, it can really offer good money. You can at this point have an aggressive trading approach: stay invested, try to buy on dips using different methods that I will teach you. Same approach — aggressive — because it's only two bases old. But once you're three bases old, I don't have the same approach anymore.

## Resetting the Count

For the whole count to reset, the price has to break the low of the lowest point of the previous resting period. In one case it was 293 — and it broke, and the whole count reset from there.

Now if the stock starts going higher again, we will reset the whole count again: first, second, third — and again it collapsed. After the third the upside was limited. This is the false breakout setup. If you don't do this base count you would attempt that stock, because it looks really tight — but I will never attempt it there. In fact, it just did not work; another failure.

Now it has again done the reset. The whole count is reset again. If it goes higher, I will start the count again from the beginning.

## Summary

If I see a stock in an uptrend and I'm thinking of putting it on my watchlist, I will just have a quick glance to see how many bases old it is. If it is really extended — three or four bases old already — I will let it pass and not attempt it.
