Hi guys, welcome to the new chapter: **Ride or Sell**.

In the previous chapters, you learned how to scan, how to make a watchlist, and how to finally act on that watchlist. But this is the trickiest part of the business.

When should you sell? Should you ride the trade or close it?

When you buy a stock, and your stop is placed, the losing side is clear. You know where your exit point is if the trade is wrong. Losses do not create the same confusion because the stop tells you what to do.

The confusion begins when you are in profit.

Ask yourself: when you are making money, are you not confused about whether to ride it or close it? You are scared that if you do not close it, you will lose the gains you are seeing.

In some trades, the stock keeps going higher after you sell, and then you regret selling early. On the next trade, you decide that this time you will sit tight. But that next trade goes up and then never goes higher from there.

Because of this randomness, you never feel as if you have a clear pattern or clear direction on whether to ride or sell.

That is what this chapter is meant to solve.

Make no mistake: the sell side takes time. From my experience of interacting with many traders who started new and later became better, all of them agree that the sell side took a long time.

Even after you improve, you never become perfect.

The only day you become perfect at selling is the day you know the exact top, sell there, and the price drops. Can you really do that every time? Can you catch the top every time?

Absolutely not.

But you can become much better at selling and be right most of the time. The sell side gets better with every trade.

## Start with the market environment

Before deciding whether to sell or ride, go back to market environment analysis and index analysis.

You need to know where the market trend stands:

- Is the trend new?
- Is it in the middle?
- Has it been going on for a long time?

In March that year, Manas says he had strong evidence that this was the place where he should take the risk of sitting on trades and not closing them early.

The method was:

1. Check whether the first few trades are acting well.
2. Watch how the market behaves over the next few days.
3. If the behaviour, push and results are good in the first few trades, then the analysis is likely right.
4. In that situation, instead of looking to sell early, look for opportunities to buy more.

This is what helped him in early April.

He says that at that time, he was not selling anything that was in profit. Zen Technologies, Olectra, Paytm, TD Power Systems, KPIT, probable Sonata Software and other names were working. He kept buying, adding and creating more positions where the trades allowed.

The approach was clear:

The trend was young, so he was not going to sell early.

If there were genuine trading opportunities, he would add to winners. Not by force, but when the trade allowed it.

Zen Technologies had a cool-down, and he used the Busted method to create more positions. Similar behaviour happened in NCC, TD Power Systems, Paytm, Olectra, KPIT, probable Sonata Software and other names. There were also names like GBMA/JBMA ⚠ where many members did well.

So the first step is to analyze where the market trend stands. That removes a lot of confusion around whether you should sell in the next two or three days, or whether you should give your trades time, add if they act well, and extract as much as possible.

## The 29 March clue

Manas refers to a tweet from **29 March**.

On 29 March, he says the market had not even properly started moving yet. But in his own portfolio, around **three stocks** were already making new highs out of roughly **5-6 names** ⚠. He decided to ride them because he had decided not to interfere.

Had he interfered, he says he would not have made money.

When he says “do not interfere,” he means:

He is not going to press the sell button just because profit is visible.

He will sell only when the trailing stop-loss gets hit, or when the market stops him out.

If the market stopped him out in the next few days, he would accept that the market was still not ready. Yes, he would lose paper gains. But that is how this game works.

You risk some paper gain to reach higher. Then you risk some more paper gain to reach even higher.

The problem starts when you are not ready to risk the visible profit.

Every day you look at your account value. Today the positions show profit. Tomorrow the profit drops by some amount. You panic and close the trade.

But what about the plan you created before entering?

If the market is in a real trend, your stocks are acting well, and they are making new highs before the market itself has fully moved, that is not the time to sell your leaders. That is the time to study the behaviour of your trades, maybe add more if the setups allow, and make the most of the good period.

## Moving to breakeven after the first strong move

Manas then explains how he rode one of these positions.

The stock had a young breakout from a massive base. The market was also suggesting that the worst was likely over after being weak for around **four months**.

He bought with around a **2% stop**, and the stop did not get hit. The stock was already up around **9% on day one**.

Because of that, he moved his stop to **breakeven** ⚠ raw: “BQN”.

He did not move it to breakeven because **9%** is a magical threshold. He moved it because if the stock was already up 9% and then hit his breakeven stop the next day, the only way that could happen would be through a massive red bar.

If the very next day prints a huge red bar after such a start, the whole setup becomes ugly and confused: one day up strongly, next day completely down. In that situation, there is no point staying in the trade.

So the breakeven stop protected him from losing money on the trade.

Yes, someone may ask: what about the **8%** gain you could lose?

His answer is that if you have already analyzed that the stock is young and the market is young, and you are still scared to lose that 8%, then how will you reach the bigger move?

He says he got many messages from people who bought at the right time, made gains in a day or two, got scared of giving back those small gains, closed the trade, and later regretted it because many names went up strongly over the next **3-4 months**.

So he was clear: he would not interfere. He moved the stop to breakeven, and the next day the stock went higher and made a new high. He still did not raise the stop aggressively. The stop was still around cost.

## Think in R, not in one visible gain

At this point, the stock may have been around **256** while the stop was much lower. Many people would think the stop is too low.

But Manas says he was thinking in terms of big R-multiple trades. When he says **1:50**, he is talking about risk. A **1R** risk can become a **50R** trade.

If you catch a **1:50** trade, you have not just made money. You have created room to take many future risks.

He says it is like making money to afford **15 new mistakes** or **15 new risks**. If you become good at the business, you will not make all those mistakes. And if you catch two or three similar trades, imagine the impact on your portfolio.

That is why he was not looking to sell. He wanted the market to stop him out because the move was still young. He was thinking of at least a **40-50%** move, and he even added more.

## Trailing with the 20-DMA

The next line he watches is the **20-day moving average**.

On one day, the value of the 20-DMA was around **201**, while his original stop was around **210**. The 20-DMA was still below his stop.

In the next few days, the 20-DMA rose above his original stop. When the 20-DMA crossed above the original stop, he moved the stop to the line.

But there is a catch:

This trailing stop works on a **closing basis**.

The stock has to close below the line in a convincing manner. A tiny close below the line or a minor touch is not enough.

As price goes higher, the moving average also rises. His stop had moved from around **214** to the 20-DMA line on a closing basis.

But there is another problem. While waiting for a convincing close below the 20-DMA, what if the stock suddenly falls sharply? What if it drops to **208**, **240** or **220** before you act?

Then you can lose a large part of the gain.

So he uses two ideas together:

1. A trailing line, such as the 20-DMA, on a convincing closing basis.
2. An emergency stop below a recent swing to protect against a sharp fall.

The reason is simple: he wants to see how the stock reacts around the 20-DMA, especially when it touches the line for the first time after a strong rally.

Sometimes the stock touches and goes. Sometimes it closes slightly below and then resumes. Sometimes it breaks badly.

To see the reaction, you need to give it room. But while giving room, you also need an emergency stop that protects you from a drastic fall.

## The emergency stop

At one point, the stock was around **272**, and the 20-DMA was around **252**. Since the start of the rally, price had not even touched that line.

Manas wanted to see what would happen the first time price came to that line.

This is similar to the Busted method: when price comes to an important reaction level, you give it **2-3 bars** to show the reaction. If the reaction is good, you may even add. If it fails badly, the emergency stop saves you.

Because he was in a good gain, the stock was young, and the market had just turned, he did not want to interfere too early.

So he placed an emergency stop below the latest swing.

If that emergency stop gets hit, there is no second guessing. He exits.

The stock came down, reacted and bounced. As the 20-DMA rose and price moved higher, he could also move the emergency stop higher, slightly below newer swings.

## When the base count gets old

At some point, the rally became **four bases old**. Manas had explained in the previous chapter that when a rally is already several bases old, the upside becomes limited.

At that point, he took out some profit.

He knew that to reset the whole base count, the stock had to come down and take the low of the most recent swing. Whatever he sold, he decided he would not add back until that reset happened.

He also posted a video in the members’ club saying the same thing: the base count needed to reset before he would add more.

Later, the stock stopped respecting the 20-DMA. This is important: there comes a point where no moving average helps because a lot of money has already been made, the move is old, and the upside is limited.

The line stops acting as a good reaction zone. Price goes below it. The reset happens.

After that reset, he later created another position when momentum again picked up. Then he again used the same logic: trail when in good gain, use the line on a closing basis, and use an emergency stop.

## When you must stop riding

The second part of the chapter is this:

There comes a point when you should not ride anymore.

There comes a point when you stop trailing with moving-average lines, interfere, and sell at market.

There are two situations:

1. **Do not interfere:** the stock is young, market is supportive, and you let the trailing stop or emergency stop take you out.
2. **Interfere:** the stock gets super-extended, the move is old, warning signs appear, and you sell into strength.

Manas gives the Zen Technologies example. He sold at market around **₹340** because the stock was four bases old, the reset was likely, and the stock had stopped respecting the line.

That is when he interfered. He did not wait for the trailing stop.

## Warning signs of extension

In one rally, out of around **eight days**, there were **seven green days**.

That was the first warning.

The angle of the rise also changed. Earlier, the angle was around **43 degrees** or **63 degrees**. Toward the end, the last two days were almost **84 degrees**, close to vertical.

When the rally is no longer young, and the angle starts turning toward **90 degrees**, and there are many green days in a row, that is a warning.

If this happens after the stock has already extended once before, it is an even bigger warning.

This was the second extension in a short time. Manas sold almost the entire position and stopped. After that, the stock did not care about the moving-average line and broke down.

So the summary for this first case is:

- Buy early in a young move.
- Move stop to breakeven only when the chart logic supports it.
- Trail with the 20-DMA on a convincing closing basis.
- Keep an emergency stop below recent swings.
- Do not interfere while the move is young.
- Interfere and sell into strength when the move is old, extended, steep, and full of warning signs.

The warning signs include:

- rally is already **three or four bases old**;
- stock is already up around **50%** from the move or **30%** from the latest base;
- angle shifts toward **90 degrees**;
- many green days in a row;
- high volume appears near the top;
- extension happens for the second time in a short period.

## NCC: selling into strength after the warnings

The next example is **NCC**.

Manas had started buying around the same time and already had about **seven positions** in the stock, using different entry methods covered earlier.

He rode the move. Once the 20-DMA crossed his original stop, he started trailing with the line on a closing basis.

The stock touched the line once and bounced. This is why closing basis matters: if you exit merely because price touches the line intraday, you may get thrown out too early.

By around **2 May**, he had around **seven positions** and roughly **78,000 shares** ⚠. On **3 May**, he sold for the first time.

Why?

Because the warning signs were visible.

The stock had broken out from a base and was now around **27% up** in only a few weeks, from **6 March to 2 May**. It was not a young move anymore.

Second, toward the end of the rally, the stock printed many green bars together for the first time.

Third, the angle changed. Earlier the stock had been rising steadily. In those last few days, the slope changed sharply, like the stock shifted from third gear to fifth gear.

Then it formed a mini base and broke out again, but the angle remained worrisome and close to vertical.

This is when he stopped trailing with the moving average and sold into strength.

On **3 May**, he sold around **₹125**. That day, price went higher but could not sustain and closed weak.

On **5 May**, he sold more because the stock struggled for two days to break out. He did not even want that breakout because the warning signs were already there.

On **8 May**, when the stock broke the low of the mini base, he sold the majority / almost the entire remaining position.

That is how he captured the move.

He stayed early because market conditions supported it. NCC was a leader. It was making highs even when the broader market was weak.

But when the warning signs appeared, he sold into strength.

The art of selling is often this: sell when everyone else is buying. Sell to the latecomers who enter after the stock is on news channels and making highs.

## TD Power Systems: when 20 becomes too loose

The next example is **TD Power Systems**.

Manas bought it on **24 April** at around **₹161**.

On the index, **24 April** was still a young market. There was no major overheating yet. So he had bought a good stock in a young market.

His view was clear:

He would not sell early. The only exits were:

- trailing stop-loss getting hit;
- the stock getting super-extended, forcing him to interfere and sell at market.

As the stock moved up, his trailing stop rose with the moving-average line. He also kept an emergency stop slightly below a swing.

The stock continued higher and had not even touched the moving-average line during the rally. Since he did not know how the stock would react the first time it came back to the line, he wanted to watch the reaction.

At one point, the stock had three down days in a row, and he added more because it gave a reversal opportunity in a good stock. The next day was a Strong Start, and the stock moved higher again.

Then he analyzed where TD Power Systems stood.

It had broken out from a big base. From the base, it was around **33% up**. It was not young anymore.

Then came the next warning: many green bars in a row for the first time in the rally, toward the end, when the stock was already around **30%** up.

The angle of rise also changed.

That is when he took some profits.

When a stock is already extended and has multiple warning signs, you do not keep trailing with the 20-DMA as if the move is still young. The 20-DMA may be too deep. By the time it breaks, you may have given back too much.

He suggests taking partial profits — roughly **50-70%** ⚠ depending on the situation — and then managing the remaining position more closely.

For the remaining position, you can:

- trail with the **10-DMA** instead of the 20-DMA;
- move the stop below the mini base;
- move the stop below the low of the mini base if you are very risk-averse.

But this tighter protection is only for an old move.

If you use it when the move has just started, you will choke the trade.

When the move is old, protecting aggressively makes sense. When the move is young, protecting too tightly kills the trade before it can become meaningful.

For traders using the 10-DMA, the logic is the same as the 20-DMA:

- use it on a closing basis;
- require a convincing close;
- keep an emergency stop slightly below;
- give the stock some room to show reaction.

In the TD Power Systems example, the stock did not close convincingly below the 10-DMA and went tight. That is what real trading looks like: not perfect, not textbook-clean, but manageable if your rules are clear.

## Olectra: selling 75% into strength

The next example is **Olectra** ⚠.

Manas bought it around **₹682**, and the stock took off.

At the beginning, many green days in a row were not a worry because it was the start of the move. When a stock breaks out from a big base and starts strongly, that can be a good sign. That is what real strength often looks like.

At the same time, he was trailing with the moving-average line on a closing basis and using an emergency stop slightly below the line, so the stock had room to show its reaction.

But after resting only **four or five days**, it got extended again.

Now there were again four or five green bars in a row. The bar size increased. Volume became very large.

From the base, the stock was around **27%** up, maybe closer to **30%**. It was not young anymore.

This second extension after a short rest was a warning.

The market context also mattered. Around **14-15 June**, the market was already overheated and around **one, two, three bases old**.

So he combined the reasons:

- stock was already up around **30%** from the base;
- many green bars appeared for the second time;
- bars were larger;
- volume was very high near the top;
- the broader market was overheated;
- the angle was around **80 degrees**.

Because of this, he sold **75%** at around **9:40** on **13 June**, the large day.

After that, the stock went only around another **4%** and then remained around that area. There was a double-top type behaviour. He says you will not always catch the exact top like he did in Zen Technologies or NCC, but you can often exit before deep trouble begins.

This is selling into strength:

When everyone is buying, and you can see signs that the stock is topping out and the move is exhausted, you sell before it gets into trouble.

Sometimes the stock may still go higher. That will happen. The job is not to capture every last rupee. The job is to consistently take out as much as the move allows.

## MRPL: 22% in three days

The next trade is **MRPL**.

Manas bought around **₹66.45**, and the stock broke out well.

On **19 June**, he sold around **60%** of the position ⚠ raw: “sold 607”.

Why?

Because the stock was up **22% in only three days**.

From the foundation chapters, he had studied how stocks usually move:

- often **three to four / three to five bars** in one direction;
- average move often around **15-20%**;
- history sometimes shows **22%** or **35%** type moves.

Here, the stock had already reached the upper end very quickly. So he took out some profit.

The broader market also mattered. Around **16 June**, the market was no longer young. If that index chart were a stock chart, it would look super-extended. The angle had changed.

So his approach had changed. He was no longer aggressively riding everything the way he had in March, April and May. He was now protecting profits earlier.

This MRPL case did not have every warning from the previous examples. It was not up **30-35%** from a big base, and it was not a second extension. It was still the first breakout.

But it had already moved **22% in three days**, and the broader market was extended. That was enough reason to sell around **60%**, keep around **40%**, and manage the rest more tightly.

For the remaining position, he stopped trailing with the 20-DMA because the market was too extended. A deeper stop made no sense in a risky market.

He switched to the **10-period moving average** with the same emergency stop logic.

If the stock closed slightly below the 10-DMA but did not hit the emergency stop, he would not close immediately. He had seen stocks stay around the line and then resume.

The emergency stop would keep the position alive and could even offer another entry if the stock reacted well.

The key point:

When the market weather changes, the trade-management approach changes.

In March, April and May, he could be aggressive. Later, when the market became extended, he became defensive. He protected profits closely because if the market starts falling, many stocks — even good-looking setups — will follow. He says around **75%** of stocks follow the market.

In a bad market, it makes no sense to trail with deep stops.

## JBM Auto: using 5-DMA for the last piece

The next example is **JBM Auto**.

This was a five-star trade taken by a few members in the club.

The position was created, and the stock immediately gave good results: around **9% up**. In the next few days, it made new highs.

Then, for the first time, such a strong stock came close to the **20-DMA**.

When a strong stock comes near the 20-DMA for the first time, Manas wants to see whether that area becomes a Busted zone — a place where the stock reacts and allows adding more.

Instead of worrying that the stock is coming down, he says he is happy it is coming down because it may let him buy more of a strong stock. Unless weak hands get flushed out, the stock may not give another entry.

The stock had moved from around **₹662** to **₹845** in a matter of days.

It then came down near the 20-DMA. Because there was an emergency stop in place, the members did not have to panic.

The stock bounced, but one day it closed badly. Since it was the first close near that area and the emergency stop was not hit, he did not want to close on day one itself.

In the next few days, the stock went very tight. He calls this **no follow-through sell** / panic selling: one bad day did not negate the whole structure because the stock tightened instead of collapsing.

Then it came back above the 20-DMA. The emergency stop saved members from closing the position early. In fact, they added more when the stock proved itself.

It behaved like Zen Technologies: it threw weak hands out, went under the 20-DMA, came back above it, and got supercharged again.

But then the question changed:

Is this still a young move?

From one point, the stock was up around **82%**. From the latest base, it was up around **26%**. By no means was it young anymore.

Also, this was around **21 June**, when the broader market was no longer young.

At that point, there were a few choices:

1. Sell at market and take profit on at least **50-70%**.
2. Trail with the **10-DMA** instead of the 20-DMA.
3. Use a combination: take some profit off and trail a smaller remaining piece with the **5-DMA**.

Manas prefers the combination in this situation.

He would not recommend holding the full position here. When the move was young, members held the full position. But here, the move was old.

For the remaining quantity — maybe the last **20-30%** — trailing with the **5-DMA** can work in a very strong market. The same rules apply:

- use closing basis;
- keep an emergency stop;
- if the stock closes below the line for the first time but the bars are small, give it some room.

In this case, after the first close below the 5-DMA, the stock bounced the next day with high volume, and the trade was back in profit.

Some stocks in strong markets can stay extended for a long time. For that last smaller piece, the 5-DMA trailing method can help.

But later the stock became extremely extended. The angle became almost straight in the last three days. Members sold whatever was left; they sold around **90% of the leftover** and then the last piece.

The stock had about **14 bars in a row**, and the last two bars had the largest volume since the start of the rally. That is where you sell into strength and stop trading it.

By **30 June**, Manas says the upside in the coming days was very limited.

This kind of move, volume and size near the top usually kills the upside.

## The emergency-stop concept again

Manas then explains the emergency-stop concept again.

Imagine you take a trade around **₹458**, and the stock goes higher. This is in May, when the market is still young. So your decision is clear:

You are not selling early. You will sell only if:

- the stock gets super-extended; or
- the trailing stop gets hit.

The trailing stop is the moving-average line. The stock comes down toward the line for the first time.

He wants to see how it reacts.

So he keeps an emergency stop below the relevant level and gives the stock room.

The stock touches the line but does not close below it. After a few days, it closes slightly below the line. Many people would close the trade immediately because they learned that a close below the moving average means sell.

But trading is not that perfect.

Minor closes below the line are acceptable when:

- the emergency stop is intact;
- volume is not alarming;
- the bar size is not large;
- the move is still young;
- the stock is coming to the line for the first time.

In the example, the next day the stock came back above the line, went tight, formed an inside bar, and offered another entry.

Then it resumed and went up another **27%**.

If you had closed just because of a minor close below the line, you would have missed the move.

This is the flexibility he wants:

Have a clear emergency stop rule, but be flexible with the moving-average line when the move is young and the stock is testing the line for the first time.

Strong stocks can bounce from the 20-DMA even after minor closes below it. Sometimes they close below for two or three days, but the selling pressure visibly slows. Then they resume.

One bounce may look small on the chart but can still be around **13%**.

So the rule is:

Be flexible with trailing lines when the move is young.

Be much less flexible when the move is old and warning signs appear.

When the move becomes extended, green bars repeat, angle changes, volume expands near the top, and the stock stops respecting moving averages, be smart and sell before it is too late.

Some extended stocks eventually stop respecting every moving average and collapse. One example fell around **30%** and then did nothing for many months.

## Final summary

The sell side is the trickiest part because there is confusion when you are riding a profitable trade.

That confusion is normal in the beginning. With every trade, it gets better. Every trade becomes a case study.

Keep these points in mind:

Every day, know what kind of market you are in.

Know where the market trend stands.

If the trend is late, use a defensive approach. Take quicker profits. If you buy today and get a good move in the next two days, take the gain instead of dreaming about a giant move in an old market.

Manas gives a recent example where he bought a setup that had a big base breakout, similar to how Zen Technologies had looked when it started. But he had already sold the majority — around **70-90%** — because the broader market was no longer young.

The setup was good, but the weather was different.

He waited for big gains in Zen Technologies because the overall weather supported it. In the later market, he was not waiting for big gains because the environment was different.

In a bad environment, the majority of stocks follow the market. Even great setups do not work smoothly.

For big moves like JBM Auto, probable Mazdock, RVNL and similar names, you need market support.

So in the current market, he was taking smaller gains and booking early.

The final lesson:

Know the market weather every day. Based on that, decide whether to ride or take quick gains. That one decision makes life much easier.
