## Why journaling matters

Manas opens with a follower’s tweet: for the first time, after he started tracking his trades, he had a double-digit increase in his portfolio in a month.

That is important because of a simple principle:

> What you measure gets monitored.

If you start measuring and noting your performance, whether good or bad, you can actually see where you are going wrong. Without writing it down, your mind cannot honestly hold the exact metrics of many trades. Too many trades, too many emotions, too many “I think I did okay” stories. The brain is a very talented CA when it is calculating excuses.

A lot of people treat journaling casually. Either they do not maintain a journal at all, or they update it once a month. With that level of casual behaviour, you will not really know what went wrong during the month. Usually, only after a big hit do people realise that they should have been monitoring and logging every trade.

Manas says he has not seen anyone doing really well who has no idea how much he is making. Everyone who is serious is maintaining a log in some form.

You are free to create your own log sheet. Manas can only show the tips and the sheet he uses.

## What a trading journal records

As traders, we often get caught up in the excitement of finding the next trade and forget to track the trades already taken. Every day, the focus is on the next setup for tomorrow. But if you are not maintaining a log sheet, you do not even know whether the method you are following is working.

Without knowing whether the method works, you keep looking for new trades with the same method and wonder why there is no progress.

A trading journal is a record of all your trades.

At the basic level, you record:

- quantity;
- entry point;
- exit point;
- whether the trade was intraday or delivery;
- stop-loss;
- holding period;
- capital allocation;
- risk taken;
- profit or loss;
- win rate;
- losing rate;
- average gain;
- average loss.

A good journal should also include a few words on the trade:

- Why did you take it?
- What exactly did you see?
- Was there any pattern?
- What was the setup called?
- What mistake, if any, did you make?

If several trades were taken on the same pattern, club them together and check the success rate of that pattern. This is how you stop treating every trade like a separate emotional TV serial and start seeing the system.

Manas also repeats the idea from the previous chapter: maintain a mistake sheet. Write down all mistakes, no matter how big or small. The goal is to have fewer mistakes in that sheet over time.

If you are new, or struggling in your first year of trading, you may feel lost and dejected. But if you journal every trade and also record the mental issues you faced during those trades, you are sitting on your best trading book.

Usually, traders look outside. They read books by great traders and try to learn from them. But your own trading journal is also a book. If you study your mistakes honestly, you may not even need to wait for one full year to improve.

Every 20-30 days, or after every 10-15 trades, study the sheet. You will see patterns in your mistakes.

Most people keep looking for new trades and new gains. But if you focus on reducing mistakes, that also improves the bottom line. Profit is not only made by finding more upside. It is also made by plugging the holes through which money is already leaking.

Do not update the journal once in 20 days from memory. By then, you will not remember exactly what you were thinking, why you closed the trade, or what emotion was driving you.

Update it right away if possible. If not right away, then at least in the evening. The raw transcript has a short garble here — “what’s marked the role?” ⚠ — but the intended instruction is clear: log the trade while the memory is still fresh.

## Allocation: the column most casual traders ignore

Allocation means the amount of money invested in the stock out of the total capital.

For example, if the account is **₹10 lakh** and the trade uses about **₹1.71 lakh**, the allocation is roughly **17%**.

The sheet calculates current allocation automatically, but Manas also manually writes the **original allocation** and **original risk**.

Why manually? Because a single trade may later be split into multiple rows when exits happen on different dates.

In one sample, the same stock — **Bajaj** — was bought on **2 August**, but sold on three different dates. Because of that, the original entry is split into three rows:

- one part sold the same day;
- one part sold on the second day;
- one part sold on the third day.

The holding period differs for each row, so the rows need to be separate.

But the trade itself was originally one trade, not three.

That is why he writes original allocation separately. In the Bajaj example, the total original allocation comes to **14.6%**, so he records **14.6%** as the original allocation.

At the time of buying Bajaj, he did not know whether he would sell it in one part, three parts, five parts or ten parts. The original trade was one decision. The later rows are only exit splits.

If he simply counted rows, the sheet might show something like **25-27** trades, or later **28** rows in the sample. But that would be wrong if several rows belong to the same original trade.

For performance review, Bajaj should count as one trade, not three.

The same logic applies to **ITI**. In the sample, ITI was bought on **3 August** and sold on two different dates, so the sheet has one intraday-style entry and one delivery entry. The original allocation is **17.2%**, and the original risk is **0.36%**.

Manas also points to a sample mismatch where a manually entered original-risk value should match the actual original risk. The raw transcript says “0.21” and then “it should be 0.15” / “1.15” ⚠. Because this is number-sensitive, it should be checked against the sheet before the final book version.

## How the sheet answers performance questions

The first job is entering the trades properly. The second job is using the data.

At the end of the month, Manas counts how many original trades he took.

In the sample, he has taken **18 trades**.

If he simply totals all visible rows, the sheet shows **28** entries. But those are not 28 independent trades because some trades were split across multiple exit rows.

Then he filters winners and losers.

For winners, he filters the return column for anything greater than zero.

In the sample:

- total original trades: **18**;
- winners: **11**;
- losers: **7**;
- win rate: **61%**;
- losing rate: **39%**.

Then he checks the average winner.

If the average winning trade returns **3.82%** on investment, it means that when he buys at ₹100 and the trade is a winner, he is selling around **₹103.82** on average.

Then he filters losers by changing the filter from greater than zero to less than zero.

In the sample, the average losing trade is about **0.71%**.

This is where journaling becomes a trading tool, not a school homework notebook.

If you know your win rate, average win size and average loss size, you can judge whether the system has an edge. If you only know “I took many trades, boss,” you have a diary, not data.

## Using the journal to study market conditions

Manas then asks: in what market conditions do you get really good trades?

By “really good trades,” he means trades that give meaningful moves — for example **10%** moves. In the sample sheet, he uses **5%** as the threshold because he had not entered many 10% examples in the sample data.

If you have one year of trading data, you can ask:

- When did the easiest period occur?
- When were you getting back-to-back 10% or 5% moves?
- What was the market breadth at that time?

His process:

1. Filter all trades.
2. Mark anything above the selected threshold — in the sample, **5%** — as a decent winner.
3. Take each winner one by one.
4. Go to the market sheet.
5. Check market breadth on the entry date or around the trade period.

In the sample, **DCW** is one winner.

He checks the market sheet for **4 August**. He stresses that the date is sample data, not an accurate historical claim.

On that date, the number of shares above the 20-DMA was very high — the raw transcript says “more than 900,000” ⚠, which is almost certainly an ASR/formatting problem and should be checked against the actual sheet. The teaching point is that market health was on the higher side.

In that environment, you get bigger moves.

Another sample winner is recorded as “7% manure” ⚠ on **13 August**. The stock name is garbled, but the lesson is the same: on that date also, the number of shares above the 20-DMA was on the higher side.

With this information, you can identify easy conditions and difficult conditions.

When breadth is not strong, you may not get 10% moves. You may get only **3%**, **4%** or **5%** moves.

That changes your selling tactics.

Markets are dynamic. They keep changing. You cannot trade the same way throughout the year and expect the same result. If you do not adjust, you will not get the results you are looking for.

This insight is possible only because the trade entries were recorded.

## Average allocation and average risk

From the original-allocation column, Manas can see his average allocation.

In the sample, the average allocation is **16.25%**.

From the original-risk column, he can see his average account risk per trade.

In the sample, he is risking about **0.27%** of the account on average per trade.

This matters because allocation is everything.

If you find a really good setup and execute it well, but you do not allocate enough money, the trade will not move your account meaningfully.

On the other hand, an average chart reader with only decent technical-analysis knowledge may still make more money if he:

- records his performance;
- knows where he stands every day;
- understands his average allocation;
- understands his average risk;
- takes calculated risk when the setup and environment allow it.

If the trade works, he makes more money than the better analyst who had the right idea but placed a tiny position as if the stock was a suspicious samosa from a railway platform.

That is the main point of the sheet:

Maintain the information. Know your performance. Know your allocation. Know your risk. Know the market conditions in which your method works best.

If you are not entering this data, Manas says you are not treating trading as a business. You are doing it in the hope of making money.

Hope is not a performance metric.
