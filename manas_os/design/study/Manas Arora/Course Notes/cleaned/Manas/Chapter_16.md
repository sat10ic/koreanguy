This video is a live watchlist build.

The point is not only to show which names make the list. The point is to show how names are rejected, because most watchlist mistakes come from getting excited by one neat daily chart and forgetting to ask whether the weekly chart is already tired.

Manas starts with the **strong-group list** made in MarketSmith. He repeats the rule from the daily-routine chapter:

If he finds **5-7 good names** from this strong-group list, he stops there. He does not go to other scans just because the market has more shiny objects available.

For this task he uses the **two-screen layout**:

- weekly chart;
- daily chart.

The four-screen layout is not required while making the watchlist. Weekly plus daily is enough to answer the main question: is this stock still young enough to offer a meaningful move, or has it already done the easy part while you were making tea?

## Start with the strong-group list

He sorts the list by how the stocks performed that day, in ascending order.

If a name is down around **5%** and is not tight at all, he does not waste time on it. He scrolls down toward names that are under roughly **2%** and then begins checking the setup.

The first test is on the daily chart:

- Is the buying force good?
- Is the stock tight for the last **4-5 days**?
- Are there multiple purple dots?
- Are the last few bars small relative to the earlier big bars?

One candidate has about **42%** buying force, multiple purple dots, and tight daily action for the last **4-5 days**. On the daily chart, it looks neat.

But the weekly chart changes the story.

The stock has been running for about **three straight weeks**. If the current week is included, there has been only minor profit booking. In the raw transcript, Manas says “not a lot of weekends got out here”; the likely intended phrase is **not a lot of weak hands got out here**. In plain trading terms, the pullback has not flushed out enough sellers.

The stock is already around **50% up** and has not really rested.

This is the mistake many traders make: they focus only on the daily chart, see the neat compression and purple dots, and select the name. Manas says to ask a harder question:

> What are the odds of getting another 50% from here?

If you are trading only for **5-10%** moves, maybe you can try it. You may even get that 5%. But if you want the peaceful system he teaches, where the aim is to catch **50%, 60%, 70%** type moves, you should be buying when the move has just started out of a large base, not when the stock is already up in the air.

Big money usually enters when the stock is basing. Chasers enter after the stock has already run for weeks.

The objective is to get involved near the start of the move, when the stock is coming out of a proper base and moving into your radar. That is where you can build meaningful position size. Not after the weekly chart is already stretched.

This candidate is also about **17% away from the 10-week average**, which becomes another reason to disqualify it.

## Nitin Spinners: low daily force, choppy structure, extended weekly

The next example is **Nitin Spinners**.

On the daily chart, it is only about **12% up**, but it is not neat. It is choppy. It does not respect the **10-DMA** and **20-DMA** well, and it is not even respecting the **50-DMA** cleanly. It has no force.

Manas says he is sure he can find a much better, more linear name.

There is a possible reversal or Busted opportunity for tomorrow, and there is some force, but the weekly again creates a problem: the stock is about **58% up** over the recent move.

He asks students to study what usually happens after a **58-60%** move. How many days does the stock stay dead? How long does it need to rest before the next serious move?

One way to avoid extended names is to look at the **slope of the moving averages**. If the moving averages are climbing close to a **90-degree angle** for an extended period, the stock is usually not in a young move anymore. It is extended.

The position should have been built when the setup still looked ugly and was just beginning to break out, or when the stock had just started moving. Not after the moving-average slope has been steep for many days.

He compares this idea with a prior trade `[FLAG raw: "prequired trade"; exact ticker/trade name unclear]`, saying that the earlier trade was a young trend when he built the position. This current example is no longer young.

He also compares it with **Zen Technologies** moving from around **200 to 350**. Zen Tech gave four red reversal days, but the reversal did not work because it came after the stock was already up a lot. At that point, the buying had already been used. Big players were not interested in that shallow pullback; they wanted a deeper correction.

The lesson is simple: a reversal or Busted entry after a large extended move is much lower quality than the same pattern appearing early in a fresh trend.

Manas says every stock should be bought with the mindset that it could become a **100R trade** `[FLAG raw: "100 hour trade"; likely "100R trade"]`. That kind of trade usually comes when the stock is coming out of a U-shaped base and you buy near the start. Zen Tech came out of its base and he started adding there. He sold later, not started buying later.

## Stop after 6-7 names

Once he already has **six-seven names**, he stops. He does not keep checking endlessly.

That is his watchlist.

He closes with a warning: do not become excited and buy a trade tomorrow without going through this video, the previous watchlist videos, and the process of making the right selection.

If you buy stocks that are super-extended, you are usually playing for very small gains, and it is very likely you will start with a losing trade.

His instruction is to finish all **six-seven watchlist videos** in the library if you are not sure how to make the watchlist. If there are doubts, use the members-club channel and ask.

The chapter's core lesson is not “find the prettiest daily chart.” It is:

1. start with strong groups;
2. shortlist only **5-7** names;
3. check daily force, tightness, and purple dots;
4. verify that the weekly is young or properly rested;
5. reject extended names even if the daily chart looks tempting;
6. stop once the list is enough.
