Hi guys, welcome to the next chapter: **Drawdowns**.

This is one of the most important chapters because this is where many traders, especially in the initial stages, struggle. In a drawdown, you start acting very differently from how you act when the account is going up.

Before discussing how Manas manages drawdown periods, first understand what a drawdown is.

In simple terms, imagine your account value rises from one point to a higher point. Then it dips. Later it goes higher again, then dips again, then rises again.

Every time the account drops from a previous peak, that drop is called a **drawdown**.

When your account value is falling from its peak, you are in a drawdown period.

Can you escape drawdowns completely?

Absolutely not.

There is no way you will buy something and it will only keep going higher and higher. Every new trader and every seasoned trader goes through drawdowns.

The difference is control.

A seasoned trader controls the drawdown. He knows where to draw the line.

A new trader often has no idea what drawdown is, where to apply brakes, or where to slow down. He continues to trade the same way and ends up losing a lot of money. By the time he realises how much he has lost, it is already too late.

If you have traded for a few months or years, you will relate to this: your behaviour is different when you are in a drawdown versus when your account is going up.

By behaviour, Manas means:

- execution;
- thinking;
- risk-taking;
- patience;
- interference with trades.

Everything changes when you are losing money.

## Clear rules are non-negotiable

To control a losing period, you need clear-cut rules.

Not vague rules. Not “I’ll be careful from tomorrow” rules. Proper rules where there is no confusion and no second-guessing.

One rule Manas suggests is this:

If you are down **3%** from the top, reduce size.

Do not get lost in the exact number. You can create your own rule. But as an example, suppose your account reaches **₹10 lakh** and then drops **3%** from that top.

At that point, the first rule kicks in.

For the next few trades, you take only half your usual size.

If you were risking **1%** per trade, now you risk only **0.5%** per trade.

Why?

Because one of two things is probably happening:

- the market is bad, which is why you are down;
- or you are not in sync with the market.

Maybe your head is not straight. Maybe you are not taking the right setups. Maybe FOMO has arrived wearing sunglasses and calling itself confidence.

Whatever the reason, things are not going well.

So you apply brakes.

## The 5% stop-trading rule

If after that you lose another **2%**, meaning you are now down a total of **5%** from the top, Manas says you should completely suspend trading.

Close all your trades, even if some are working, and take a break.

Disconnect from the market.

Some people interpret a break as “do not trade, but still read the market and still read charts in the evening.”

For new traders, especially those with poor emotional control, Manas suggests a complete break.

Every year, there will be bad-environment days where no matter how good a trader you are, the odds are not in your favour. Trying hard in that kind of market will not make money for you.

You also need to develop **sit-out power**.

Disconnecting from the market develops that sit-out power. It develops patience. It teaches you that you can conquer the urge to keep acting.

You are in control, not your emotions.

You control the emotions; the emotions do not control you.

At any time, by free will, you can take a break.

That sounds simple. It is not. The market’s favourite intern is your impulse.

## Protect your mental state

When you are down **5%**, your head is not in the same place.

Again, **5%** is just an example number. You may stop at **7%**, or you may stop at **3%**.

The point is that mentally you do not feel the same after losing a meaningful part of your account.

If you do not protect that mental state, things can become worse.

When you are not at peace, the next trade carries baggage. You have already lost 5%, so the next trade does not feel neutral anymore. It becomes the trade that must recover you.

That baggage forces mistakes.

Suppose you do not take a break. You take the next trade. The trade goes up and then suddenly starts coming down.

Manas says he can almost guarantee you will interfere and stop following the rules.

You may close the trade at market ⚠ and miss the gain if it later goes higher.

## The drawdown-baggage example

Imagine you are already in a drawdown.

You did not take a break. You are carrying the old baggage that your account is down.

In that period, you find a trade. It breaks out well. You are making money, and it is recovering part of your drawdown.

Then it starts dropping and comes back to your entry price.

This is where many traders stop following the plan. They close the position because they do not want to lose further. The pain is already high, so they cannot tolerate more pain.

Because of the old baggage, they are out of the trade.

They are not analysing the stock neutrally.

The stock does not know you personally.

It does not know you have baggage.

It does not know you are in the trade.

It does not know your account status, your mental state, or how you feel.

You need to look at it neutrally.

In Manas's example, the stock is down for three red bars in a row, and that too with massive red bars. But because of baggage and immaturity, the trader closes the trade.

Then the trade takes off.

The trader did not look at the chart neutrally. He looked at it through his account status and closed it.

Now, when the stock goes up, he feels worse.

First, he felt bad because he lost the open gain and failed to recover the drawdown. Then he feels worse because he did not follow the plan. He realises he could have recovered the drawdown and even made additional gains.

Reacting differently during a drawdown is your number-one enemy.

## Reduce risk, not execution quality

The purpose of this chapter is how to manage drawdowns and how to execute during drawdowns.

Your execution cannot change.

Your risk size can change.

When trades are not working, when a few trades get hit back-to-back and you are already down **3%**, reduce size.

But if the market is not extended and the environment is still good, you cannot change the execution and management just because your account is in a drawdown.

Analyse the market from the market's point of view, not from your account's point of view.

Go back to the market-environment and index-analysis lessons:

- What are the indices doing?
- What are conditions like?
- Is the market actually bad?
- Or is only your account having a bad patch?

If things are not that bad, do not change your execution just because your account is down.

In the example, if the trader had not interfered because of drawdown baggage, the trade could have recovered the drawdown and made extra gains.

## Are you here only to recover?

Ask yourself something when you are in a drawdown:

Are you thinking only about recovering the drawdown?

What if you recover it?

Is that the goal?

Are you here only to recover from drawdowns?

Suppose you buy a stock, it goes up, and you close it only because your drawdown is recovered.

You may feel happy that day. The account is back from the hole.

But later, if the stock keeps going, you feel worse. You ask why you closed the trade just because you recovered the drawdown.

The purpose is not merely to recover.

The purpose is to take good trades and make good money.

If you treat recovery as the finish line, you may exit a valid trade too early. Recovery is not a trophy. It is just the account returning from the washroom.

## How to make a comeback

How do you make a comeback, and how do you know the market is doing good again? ⚠

Take trades with very small size.

Let one or two trades work.

If they are working, put in more money.

If they are not working, maybe things are still not right for you.

Keep trying with very small size until you start getting traction and see some stocks giving good improvement.

This advice is especially for people who are just starting out or struggling.

## Do not watch the balance daily

Do not look at your balance on a day-to-day basis.

Not in a drawdown.

Not even when you are making money.

When you keep watching the balance, you attach emotions to it.

Every time you are down from that number — and you will be down from it sometimes, because no trade line goes only upward — you start acting differently.

Your execution changes.

You start interfering with your trades.

During a drawdown, you will not change your execution style if you have solid belief that it is just a matter of time.

Either you sit out patiently, or you take very small-sized trades. You know that one good trade can recover a small drawdown and put you back in green.

How do you develop that belief?

Only by taking trades, reviewing them and getting better over months.

Nothing changes overnight.

## Discipline is not new information

People who approach trading with a short-term view, thinking they can quickly start taking money from it, are underestimating the skill.

Trading is a hard skill.

It requires self-discipline.

People lose money here because of bad behaviour and poor discipline.

And discipline is not built in one or two trades, especially when every mistake costs money.

Most traders already know the advice. Manas says this is not breaking news.

You already know much of this.

But then why are many still not making money?

It is like weight loss.

Everyone knows that to lose weight, you need a good diet, a calorie deficit, and consistency.

But it is hard to follow a good diet. It is hard not to eat sugar. It is hard not to drink aerated drinks.

Because of that lack of discipline in diet, the person does not lose weight.

Trading is similar.

You know certain behaviours are not good for you. But if you are not consciously controlling them, you will struggle.

No one is coming to monitor you.

You do not have a boss here. No one is tracking your performance for you.

You have to monitor your own performance, be your own coach, and fix your behaviour issues.

That is how you control drawdown.

## How Manas controls drawdowns

When Manas hits a rough patch, he takes a break and then takes very small-sized trades.

In a bad period, he is not trying to make money.

He is trying not to lose money.

That prevents a large drop in account value.

If the bad period creates a big drop, the next good period gets wasted only recovering the bad period.

That is why drawdown control matters.

## Trailing with the rising DMA

Manas then says the same example is also a perfect illustration of trailing with the rising DMA ⚠.

Suppose you have a position and you do not interfere.

You are trailing with the 20-moving-average line on a closing basis, along with an emergency stop.

The swing low gives you an emergency stop area. The stop goes up.

On one day, the stock closes below the trailing line, but it does not hit the emergency stop.

In that situation, you may give the stock some room for a day or two, at least to see whether it is really bouncing from that line or not.

The next day, the stock bounces. In fact, it may offer a new trade.

The stock was down three days in a row on a smaller frame. Manas says there must have been some possible trade on that day.

If you were only following the 20-moving-average line mechanically, you would have closed the trade at **355**.

But if you wait for the stock to show you the reaction and maintain an emergency stop to protect against a big drop, you can still hold more position.

The stock then goes back to new highs.

This is how you stay flexible and avoid interfering with trades.

Let the stock prove whether it is really good or not.

One good trade can take you out of a negative period and put you back in green.

## Final drawdown rules

Do not change your execution style.

Do not change your management style just because your account is in drawdown.

Do not look at the balance every day.

Have clear rules for when to reduce size.

Have clear rules for when to stop.

Have clear rules for how to make a comeback.

That is how Manas manages drawdowns.

That is how he says he has survived wild, violent crashes and is still here.
